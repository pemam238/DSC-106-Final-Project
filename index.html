Definition

What is drought?

Drought isn't about how little rain a place gets, it's about how much less it gets than usual. A rainforest receiving half its normally rainfall is in drought. A desert getting its typical trickle of rain is not.

Scientists measure this gap by using the Standardized Precipiatation Index (SPI). This is a score that tells you how far below normal precipitation has fallen, regardless of climate.

Surprise

Cold places
drought too.

Because drought is defined by deficit, not heat, it can strike anywhere precipitation drops below expectaion, such as snowpack in mountain ranges, rainfall in temperate forests, even Arctic regions.

An SPI below -1 signals drought. It doesn't matter whether the thermometer reads 40°C or 4°C.

Conceptualizing Standard Precipitation Index (SPI)

Step 1

The baseline

An SPI of 0 means rainfall is exactly on par with the long-term average for that region. No surplus or deficit, just a normal year. This is the reference point everything else is measured against.

DryNormalWet

SPI = 0.0

Step 2

Above average

A positive SPI means more rain than usual fell. An SPI of +1.0 means rainfall was moderately above average. The rivers run fuller, soil holds more moisture. At +2.0, the region is experiencing unusually wet conditions, which can bring flooding risks.

SPI = 0.0

Step 3

Early warning signs

An SPI of −0.5 to −0.9 signals that rainfall is running below normal. Soil begins to dry out, vegetation stress appears, and water managers start watching reservoir levels. This is when drought is developing but not yet critical.

SPI = 0.0

Step 4

Significant drought

At −1.0 to −1.5, drought is officially underway. This level of deficit means the region has received substantially less rain than it relies on. Stream flows drop, crop yields fall, and water use restrictions typically begin. This threshold is used internationally to declare drought conditions.

SPI = 0.0

Step 5

Extreme drought

An SPI of −2.0 or below is extreme drought. This is a statistically rare event that occurs in only about 2.5% of all recorded years for any given location. At this level, groundwater is severely depleted, ecosystems are under threat, and recovery can take years even after rainfall returns to normal.

      The bottom line

      The Hidden Truth

      We only worry about
      drought where we can
      see it.

      Cracked earth. Bleached sky. A desert we already knew was dry. But the data shows drought quietly devastating cold steppes, mountain valleys, and temperate plains — places that receive no emergency declarations, no headlines, no aid.

      SPI doesn't care about temperature. It only asks one question: did enough rain fall? In millions of square kilometers of cold and temperate land, the answer is increasingly no.

      The most dangerous droughts are the ones
